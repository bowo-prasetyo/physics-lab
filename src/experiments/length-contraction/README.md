# Length Contraction Lab

## Learning sequence

1. Predict whether Earth observers measure the spaceship shorter, longer or unchanged compared with its rest length.
2. Choose the spaceship's relative speed and proper length.
3. Record both endpoint positions at the same Earth-frame time.
4. Compare the measured length with the proper length and inspect the two simultaneity slices in the spacetime diagram.
5. Explore the mathematics at three levels, from operational measurement to the inverse Lorentz transformation.

## Physical model

The spaceship is an idealized one-dimensional rod with constant proper length \(L_0\), moving inertially at constant speed \(v\) along its length. Its Lorentz factor is

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}, \qquad L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma}.
\]

The proper length is measured in the rod's rest frame. In the Earth frame, the two endpoint positions must be measured simultaneously using synchronized Earth clocks. The corresponding endpoint events are not simultaneous in the rod frame. For a front endpoint in the +x direction,

\[
\Delta t' = \gamma\left(\Delta t - \frac{v\Delta x}{c^2}\right)
= -\frac{vL_0}{c^2}.
\]

The horizontal Earth-frame simultaneity slice and the tilted ship-frame simultaneity slice therefore intersect the endpoint worldlines at different pairs of events.

## Model boundaries

This is a measurement of endpoint positions, not a photograph. Optical appearance also depends on light-travel time and can involve effects such as Terrell rotation. The model does not simulate acceleration, rotation, deformation, thickness, light propagation to a camera or the dynamics of a physical rod. A perfectly rigid body cannot be accelerated as a whole in special relativity; the experiment considers uniform inertial motion after acceleration has ended.

The proper length is the object's rest-frame length. It is not the length measured by every observer; observers who see the rod moving measure a shorter length along the direction of motion.
